<h1>The First 90 Days of Running a U.S. Business After an Investor-Visa Move</h1><p>Getting permission to enter the United States and getting a U.S. company ready to operate are two very different projects.</p><p>The immigration process may require months of planning, documentation, legal work, investment decisions, and coordination.</p><p>Then approval arrives.</p><p>And suddenly the focus changes.</p><p>Now the company actually has to function.</p><p>Money needs to move through the right accounts.</p><p>Transactions need to be recorded correctly.</p><p>The owner needs a method for paying themselves.</p><p>Taxes need to be anticipated.</p><p>Undeployed capital needs a purpose.</p><p>And management needs to understand whether the business has enough cash to execute the plan that justified the move in the first place.</p><p>This is where many international owners encounter an unexpected gap.</p><p>The immigration process may have helped establish why the business exists.</p><p>It does not automatically build the financial operating system the company needs once operations begin.</p><h2>The First 90 Days Matter More Than They Look</h2><p>The first few months of a U.S. business can feel relatively simple.</p><p>There may still be significant cash in the bank.</p><p>The team may be small.</p><p>Revenue may just be starting.</p><p>That can create the impression that sophisticated financial systems can wait.</p><p>But this is exactly when financial habits are being created.</p><p>If the owner starts by:</p><ul><li>Mixing accounts</li><li>Moving money without documentation</li><li>Paying personal expenses from the company</li><li>Making undocumented related-party transfers</li><li>Operating without a cash forecast</li><li>Treating taxes as a future problem</li></ul><p>those decisions become harder to unwind later.</p><p>A stronger approach builds the system before complexity accumulates.</p><h2>Formation Is Not the Same as Financial Operations</h2><p>A company can be legally formed and still lack almost every financial process needed to operate effectively.</p><p>Formation may give you:</p><ul><li>A legal entity</li><li>Registration documents</li><li>Ownership structure</li><li>Tax identification</li></ul><p>But daily financial operations still require decisions about:</p><ul><li>Bank accounts</li><li>Bookkeeping</li><li>Payments</li><li>Expense policies</li><li>Payroll or owner compensation</li><li>Cash reserves</li><li>Financial reporting</li><li>Tax coordination</li></ul><p>These decisions should not be improvised indefinitely.</p><h2>Keep Immigration Counsel and Finance Roles Separate</h2><p>Investor-visa entrants may work closely with immigration attorneys during the planning process.</p><p>That legal relationship remains important.</p><p>But once the business begins operating, financial questions require different professionals.</p><p>For example:</p><p><strong>Immigration counsel may address:</strong></p><p>visa eligibility, legal filings, immigration requirements, and status-related questions.</p><p><strong>The finance team may address:</strong></p><p>bookkeeping, cash flow, financial reporting, tax coordination, and treasury processes.</p><p>Garzon Meridian's Arrival CFO model makes this division explicit: immigration and legal matters remain with counsel while the firm focuses on operating finance, cash discipline, and treasury setup.</p><p>Clear boundaries reduce confusion.</p><h2>Days 0–30: Build the Financial Foundation</h2><p>The first month should focus on creating clean infrastructure.</p><p>Do not begin with elaborate forecasting software.</p><p>Start with the basic systems that every later decision will depend on.</p><h2>Map Every Entity</h2><p>International entrepreneurs may arrive with more than one business relationship already in place.</p><p>There might be:</p><ul><li>A new U.S. company</li><li>A foreign operating business</li><li>A foreign parent</li><li>Personal investment accounts</li><li>Related entities</li><li>Joint ventures</li></ul><p>Create a simple map showing who owns what.</p><p>Include:</p><ul><li>Entity names</li><li>Jurisdiction</li><li>Ownership</li><li>Relationships</li><li>Purpose</li></ul><p>This makes future money movements easier to understand.</p><h2>Decide Which Account Belongs to Which Entity</h2><p>The same discipline should apply to banking.</p><p>Document:</p><ul><li>Operating checking</li><li>Savings</li><li>Payroll accounts</li><li>Credit cards</li><li>Payment processors</li><li>Foreign accounts involved in transfers</li></ul><p>Each account should have a clear owner.</p><p>This may sound basic, but it becomes extremely important when the founder controls multiple companies.</p><p>A transfer that is obvious to the founder may be completely unclear to the accountant six months later.</p><h2>Avoid Using the Business Account as a Personal Wallet</h2><p>Closely held businesses often develop informal money habits.</p><p>The owner needs money and transfers it.</p><p>A personal purchase happens to be paid from the business card.</p><p>Another company covers an expense temporarily.</p><p>These movements may be understandable operationally.</p><p>But repeated without documentation, they can create accounting and tax confusion.</p><p>Establish a policy from the beginning.</p><p>If money moves between the owner and the business, determine what the transaction represents and record it consistently.</p><h2>Stand Up Bookkeeping Immediately</h2><p>Bookkeeping should begin when the business begins.</p><p>Not six months later.</p><p>Waiting creates an unnecessary reconstruction project.</p><p>Someone eventually has to determine:</p><ul><li>What each transfer represented</li><li>Which expense belonged to which entity</li><li>Why the owner deposited money</li><li>Which purchases were business expenses</li><li>Whether certain balances were loans or contributions</li></ul><p>Those questions are much easier to answer while the transactions are recent.</p><p>Garzon Meridian's Arrival CFO process places bookkeeping setup inside the first 30 days specifically so the books begin clean rather than being reconstructed later.</p><h2>Choose a Consistent Chart of Accounts</h2><p>A U.S. business may have different reporting needs from the owner's foreign company.</p><p>Do not simply reproduce another company's accounting structure without thinking about the U.S. operation.</p><p>The chart of accounts should make it possible to understand:</p><ul><li>Revenue sources</li><li>Major cost categories</li><li>Payroll</li><li>Owner activity</li><li>Related-party activity</li><li>Debt</li><li>Cash</li></ul><p>The exact design depends on the business.</p><p>The objective is useful reporting, not maximum complexity.</p><h2>Create a Monthly Close Process</h2><p>Even a new company should have a regular accounting rhythm.</p><p>At the end of each month:</p><ul><li>Reconcile bank accounts</li><li>Reconcile credit cards</li><li>Review transactions</li><li>Check unusual balances</li><li>Review owner activity</li><li>Produce financial statements</li></ul><p>This creates continuity.</p><p>By month six, management should not be discovering that the first five months were never properly closed.</p><h2>The First Strategic Handoff</h2><p>Once accounts and bookkeeping are structured, the company can move from simply recording activity to managing its financial position.</p><p>This is the point where a structured <a href="https://garzonmeridian.com/arrival-cfo/" target="_blank"><strong>finance setup for investor-visa business owners</strong></a> becomes particularly useful: the challenge is no longer forming the company, but building the bookkeeping, cash, owner-compensation, reporting, and treasury systems that allow the U.S. operation to run consistently from the beginning.</p><p>The link belongs here because the owner has already completed the setup stage and is now entering the operating-finance stage.</p><h2>Days 30–60: Understand Cash Before You Spend It</h2><p>The second month should introduce forward-looking financial management.</p><p>A new company may still have substantial investment capital.</p><p>That can create confidence.</p><p>But a bank balance does not tell you how long the capital will last.</p><p>For that, you need a cash model.</p><h2>Build a 13-Week Cash Forecast</h2><p>A 13-week forecast provides a short-term view of expected cash movements.</p><p>It may include:</p><h3>Cash In</h3><ul><li>Customer collections</li><li>Owner funding</li><li>Other expected receipts</li></ul><h3>Cash Out</h3><ul><li>Payroll</li><li>Rent</li><li>Vendors</li><li>Marketing</li><li>Insurance</li><li>Taxes</li><li>Professional fees</li><li>Equipment</li><li>Debt payments</li></ul><p>The model does not need to predict every dollar perfectly.</p><p>Its purpose is to show timing.</p><h2>Why Timing Matters</h2><p>A company can have enough cash for the year and still experience pressure during one particular month.</p><p>For example:</p><p>A major tax payment arrives in April.</p><p>Insurance renews in May.</p><p>A large equipment purchase happens in June.</p><p>Revenue does not increase until July.</p><p>Annual totals may look fine.</p><p>The monthly cash position may not.</p><p>That is why forecasting is more useful than simply looking at the current bank balance.</p><h2>Know Your Runway</h2><p>Runway estimates how long the company can continue operating at its current or projected rate before cash becomes constrained.</p><p>For a new U.S. business, this can be an essential metric.</p><p>If operations are being funded initially by investment capital, management should know:</p><ul><li>Current cash</li><li>Monthly burn</li><li>Expected revenue</li><li>Future obligations</li><li>Minimum reserve</li></ul><p>Runway should change as assumptions change.</p><p>It is not a permanent number.</p><h2>Separate Invested Capital From Spendable Cash</h2><p>An owner may transfer substantial capital into the U.S. operation.</p><p>That does not mean all of it should immediately be treated as available for discretionary spending.</p><p>Some may be intended for:</p><ul><li>Hiring</li><li>Inventory</li><li>Equipment</li><li>Lease deposits</li><li>Taxes</li><li>Working capital</li><li>Expansion</li></ul><p>Assigning purpose to the money makes financial decisions more disciplined.</p><h2>Establish an Owner-Compensation Policy</h2><p>One of the most important early questions is:</p><p><strong>How will the owner take money from the business?</strong></p><p>Do not leave this undefined.</p><p>The appropriate structure depends on entity type, tax circumstances, immigration considerations, and professional advice.</p><p>The operational point is simpler:</p><p>Create a documented process.</p><p>Avoid random transfers whose purpose must be reconstructed later.</p><p>Garzon Meridian includes owner compensation and related-party policy development in days 30–60 of its Arrival CFO process.</p><h2>International Owners Need Extra Discipline Around Transfers</h2><p>Cross-border founders may have legitimate reasons for moving funds between countries or related entities.</p><p>Examples might include:</p><ul><li>Capital contributions</li><li>Expense reimbursements</li><li>Intercompany services</li><li>Loans</li><li>Repayments</li></ul><p>Each transaction should have a documented business purpose and appropriate professional treatment.</p><p>Do not rely solely on bank descriptions such as:</p><p>“wire transfer.”</p><p>The accounting record should explain what the movement represented.</p><h2>Establish a Related-Party Policy Early</h2><p>If transactions will occur between connected companies, define how they will be handled.</p><p>Determine:</p><ul><li>What documentation is required</li><li>Who approves transfers</li><li>How they are recorded</li><li>How balances are reconciled</li><li>When the CPA should review them</li></ul><p>Creating the policy before transaction volume increases is much easier than reconstructing several years of activity later.</p><h2>Coordinate With the CPA During the Year</h2><p>Do not wait until tax season to introduce the company's structure to the CPA.</p><p>By then, important decisions may already have occurred.</p><p>Early coordination can help ensure that bookkeeping captures the information the tax professional will eventually need.</p><p>This is particularly valuable when ownership or transactions cross borders.</p><h2>Days 60–90: Turn the Numbers Into a Management System</h2><p>By the third month, the business should have enough activity to begin building useful reporting.</p><p>This is where the financial system should start helping the owner manage the company rather than simply document it.</p><h2>Build the First Management Dashboard</h2><p>A useful dashboard might include:</p><ul><li>Cash</li><li>Revenue</li><li>Expenses</li><li>Gross margin</li><li>Operating margin</li><li>Runway</li><li>Accounts receivable</li><li>Major obligations</li></ul><p>Not every business needs every metric.</p><p>A service company and an ecommerce business may require completely different views.</p><p>The dashboard should answer the owner's actual management questions.</p><h2>Avoid Dashboard Theater</h2><p>A dashboard with twenty attractive charts can still be useless.</p><p>The test is simple:</p><p><strong>Does this information change a decision?</strong></p><p>Useful metrics may help answer:</p><ul><li>Can we hire?</li><li>Are we spending too quickly?</li><li>Which service is profitable?</li><li>When will we need more capital?</li><li>Are customers paying slowly?</li></ul><p>If nobody knows what to do differently after seeing a metric, question whether it needs to be there.</p><h2>Compare Actual Results Against the Original Plan</h2><p>Investor-backed or investor-visa businesses often begin with a financial plan.</p><p>Once operations start, reality should be compared with that plan.</p><p>Review:</p><ul><li>Revenue</li><li>Hiring</li><li>Expenses</li><li>Capital deployment</li><li>Cash runway</li><li>Major investments</li></ul><p>The goal is not to criticize every variance.</p><p>Plans are assumptions.</p><p>The objective is to understand why reality changed.</p><h2>Turn Variances Into Decisions</h2><p>Suppose payroll is 20% above plan.</p><p>That fact alone is not necessarily bad.</p><p>Perhaps hiring occurred faster because sales exceeded expectations.</p><p>Or perhaps labor costs increased before revenue materialized.</p><p>The financial dashboard should create questions.</p><p>Then management investigates the reasons.</p><h2>Decide How Much Cash Must Remain Liquid</h2><p>By day 60–90, the business should have a better understanding of actual operating needs.</p><p>That makes it possible to begin separating cash conceptually.</p><p>For example:</p><h3>Operating Cash</h3><p>Money needed for immediate expenses.</p><h3>Reserve Cash</h3><p>Money held for uncertainty and upcoming obligations.</p><h3>Undeployed Strategic Capital</h3><p>Funds not needed immediately but still intended for future business use.</p><p>Different categories may require different liquidity characteristics.</p><h2>Do Not Chase Yield Before Defining Liquidity</h2><p>New owners can be frustrated by seeing large cash balances earning little.</p><p>But optimizing yield before understanding operating needs is backwards.</p><p>First determine:</p><ul><li>Payroll requirements</li><li>Tax reserves</li><li>Planned purchases</li><li>Growth investments</li><li>Emergency liquidity</li><li>Timing</li></ul><p>Then evaluate any funds that are genuinely not required immediately.</p><p>Garzon Meridian places treasury planning at the end of its initial 90-day sequence, after bookkeeping and cash forecasting have already been established.</p><p>That order is important.</p><h2>Treasury Decisions Need the Proper Advisory Structure</h2><p>Cash-management and investment decisions may involve regulated financial advice.</p><p>Those roles should remain clearly separated from bookkeeping and ordinary CFO work.</p><p>Garzon Meridian states that capital addressed through its treasury layer remains at third-party custodians and that investment advisory services are provided through a Registered Investment Adviser.</p><p>The broader lesson is that integrated finance does not mean ignoring professional boundaries.</p><p>It means coordinating the appropriate specialists around the same financial picture.</p><h2>The First 90 Days Should Produce a System, Not Just Documents</h2><p>By the end of the initial period, the company should ideally have more than spreadsheets.</p><p>It should have repeatable processes.</p><p>For example:</p><p><strong>Every transaction</strong> enters the books consistently.</p><p><strong>Every month</strong> closes on schedule.</p><p><strong>Every owner transfer</strong> has a classification.</p><p><strong>Every related-party movement</strong> has documentation.</p><p><strong>Every week</strong> cash can be evaluated against a forecast.</p><p><strong>Every reporting period</strong> management can see meaningful metrics.</p><p>This is what turns financial setup into infrastructure.</p><h2>Common Mistake: Waiting Until Revenue Arrives</h2><p>Some founders delay finance processes because the business has not yet generated meaningful sales.</p><p>But pre-revenue or early-stage periods can contain significant financial activity.</p><p>The company may already be spending on:</p><ul><li>Payroll</li><li>Lease</li><li>Equipment</li><li>Marketing</li><li>Professional services</li><li>Insurance</li><li>Technology</li></ul><p>Those transactions still need clean records.</p><p>Waiting until revenue becomes material simply increases the cleanup work.</p><h2>Common Mistake: Assuming the Attorney Handles Finance</h2><p>An immigration attorney may be central to the owner's move.</p><p>That does not mean they are responsible for building the company's accounting or treasury system.</p><p>Professional responsibilities should remain clear.</p><p>Use legal counsel for legal and immigration questions.</p><p>Use qualified tax professionals for tax advice.</p><p>Use appropriate financial professionals for accounting, CFO, and investment functions.</p><h2>Common Mistake: Treating Every International Wire the Same</h2><p>Cross-border transfers can represent very different things economically.</p><p>An owner contribution is not necessarily the same as an intercompany loan.</p><p>A reimbursement is not necessarily a distribution.</p><p>Recording everything under a generic “transfer” category makes future analysis difficult.</p><p>Classification should reflect the actual purpose of the transaction.</p><h2>Common Mistake: Paying the Owner Randomly</h2><p>Owners often focus on business operations first and compensation later.</p><p>Then they need personal cash and begin transferring money.</p><p>This can create ambiguity.</p><p>A documented compensation and distribution process creates cleaner accounting and gives the CPA better information.</p><h2>Common Mistake: Leaving Cash Planning Until the Balance Is Low</h2><p>A forecast is most valuable before cash becomes a problem.</p><p>If you wait until the company is almost out of money, many decisions become urgent.</p><p>With earlier visibility, management may have time to:</p><ul><li>Adjust hiring</li><li>Delay expenses</li><li>Improve collections</li><li>Raise capital</li><li>Seek financing</li></ul><p>Financial visibility expands the number of available options.</p><h2>Common Mistake: Copying the Foreign Company's Finance System</h2><p>An international founder may already operate a successful company abroad.</p><p>That experience is valuable.</p><p>But the U.S. company may have different:</p><ul><li>Accounting practices</li><li>Tax requirements</li><li>Banking</li><li>Payroll</li><li>Reporting needs</li></ul><p>Do not assume every process should simply be copied.</p><p>Build the U.S. operating stack deliberately.</p><h2>Keep Business and Personal Wealth Distinct</h2><p>The owner may have assets in several countries.</p><p>The company may also hold significant cash.</p><p>Those financial pictures interact but should not be casually mixed.</p><p>Clear separation helps with:</p><ul><li>Accounting</li><li>Planning</li><li>Documentation</li><li>Risk management</li></ul><p>Later, the owner's broader wealth plan can be coordinated with the business.</p><p>But the books should always make the distinction clear.</p><h2>Use the First Quarter to Establish Discipline</h2><p>The most valuable outcome of the first 90 days is not a perfect forecast.</p><p>It is discipline.</p><p>The company learns:</p><ul><li>Where money flows</li><li>Who approves transactions</li><li>When books close</li><li>How the owner gets paid</li><li>How taxes are coordinated</li><li>What cash is reserved</li><li>Which metrics management reviews</li></ul><p>Once those behaviors become routine, future growth is easier to manage.</p><h2>Financial Infrastructure Makes Financing Easier Later</h2><p>Companies often begin thinking about lenders only when they need capital.</p><p>But clean financial operations established from day one can make future financing preparation easier.</p><p>Reliable monthly closes, documented owner contributions, traceable sources of funds, and organized related-party activity all create a cleaner financial history.</p><p>You do not need to build the company specifically for a future loan.</p><p>You should build records that another professional can understand.</p><h2>Financial Infrastructure Also Helps With Partners and Investors</h2><p>The same principle applies if the business later:</p><ul><li>Raises outside capital</li><li>Adds a partner</li><li>Sells equity</li><li>Acquires another company</li></ul><p>Clean records reduce the amount of reconstruction required during due diligence.</p><h2>The Owner Should Be Able to See the Company Without Calling Three People</h2><p>One of the goals of a well-designed finance function is accessibility.</p><p>The owner should not need to call:</p><ul><li>The bookkeeper for cash</li><li>The CPA for taxes</li><li>The banker for reserves</li></ul><p>just to understand the basic financial position.</p><p>Different professionals will still have different responsibilities.</p><p>But management should have a coherent operating view.</p><h2>Build the Finance Function Before Complexity Arrives</h2><p>The easiest time to establish clean processes is when the company is still relatively simple.</p><p>Once there are:</p><ul><li>More employees</li><li>More accounts</li><li>More entities</li><li>More transfers</li><li>More revenue</li></ul><p>changing habits becomes harder.</p><p>The first 90 days provide a valuable window.</p><p>Use it.</p><p>Create the account structure.</p><p>Stand up bookkeeping.</p><p>Build the cash forecast.</p><p>Define owner and related-party policies.</p><p>Establish management reporting.</p><p>Then determine what capital truly needs to remain liquid.</p><p>For an international owner beginning U.S. operations, these systems can matter just as much as the original business plan.</p><p>The visa may allow the owner to enter.</p><p>The finance function helps the company operate once they arrive.</p>
