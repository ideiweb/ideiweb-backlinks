<h1>Foreign-Owned U.S. Companies: The Compliance Obligations That Still Matter</h1><p>For foreign entrepreneurs, forming a U.S. company can provide access to one of the world's largest markets, a well-established business environment, and opportunities to serve customers globally.</p><p>But forming the company is only the beginning.</p><p>Foreign ownership can create U.S. information-reporting and recordkeeping obligations that are easy to overlook—particularly when the owner lives outside the United States and the company appears relatively simple.</p><p>Recent changes to federal beneficial ownership reporting have added another layer of confusion.</p><p>U.S.-created companies are now exempt from federal Beneficial Ownership Information reporting to FinCEN. But that change did <strong>not</strong> eliminate separate IRS reporting requirements that can apply to foreign-owned U.S. entities.</p><p>Understanding that distinction is essential.</p><h2>The BOI Rule Changed — But Not Every Reporting Requirement Disappeared</h2><p>Beneficial Ownership Information reporting under the Corporate Transparency Act received significant attention when it was introduced.</p><p>The rules subsequently changed.</p><p>FinCEN first exempted U.S.-created companies through a 2025 rule change and finalized the revised BOI framework in August 2026. Under the current rule, companies created in the United States are exempt from federal BOI reporting requirements.</p><p>That may sound like foreign owners of U.S. companies no longer have significant ownership-related reporting obligations.</p><p>That conclusion can be misleading.</p><p>FinCEN reporting and IRS information reporting are separate compliance systems.</p><p>Changes to one do not automatically cancel requirements under the other.</p><h2>Form 5472 Is a Separate IRS Requirement</h2><p>One of the most important forms for certain foreign-owned U.S. businesses is <strong>Form 5472</strong>.</p><p>The IRS uses Form 5472 to collect information about reportable transactions involving certain reporting corporations and related parties.</p><p>A reporting corporation can include a U.S. corporation that is at least 25% foreign-owned. The IRS rules also specifically cover certain U.S. disregarded entities that are wholly owned by a foreign person.</p><p>This is particularly important for foreign entrepreneurs who own a single-member U.S. LLC.</p><p>For federal income tax purposes, that LLC may generally be treated as disregarded from its owner. However, the IRS treats a wholly foreign-owned U.S. disregarded entity as a separate entity for certain Section 6038A reporting requirements.</p><p>That distinction can easily be missed.</p><h2>A Simple LLC Can Still Have Complex Reporting</h2><p>A foreign owner may form a U.S. LLC and assume that limited business activity means limited compliance.</p><p>But reporting requirements depend on the entity's circumstances and transactions, not simply on whether the business feels complex.</p><p>For a foreign-owned U.S. disregarded entity subject to Form 5472 reporting, the IRS generally requires Form 5472 to be attached to a <strong>pro forma Form 1120</strong>.</p><p>This is unusual because the entity may not otherwise have a conventional corporate income tax return filing obligation.</p><p>That is exactly why the requirement can be overlooked.</p><h2>Related-Party Transactions Matter</h2><p>Form 5472 focuses heavily on transactions between a reporting corporation and related parties.</p><p>Depending on the entity and circumstances, transactions involving a foreign owner or another related person may need to be reviewed for reporting purposes.</p><p>These relationships can include transactions involving:</p><ul><li>Contributions of money to the company</li><li>Distributions or withdrawals</li><li>Payments between related entities</li><li>Loans involving owners or related parties</li><li>Certain purchases or sales</li><li>Management or service payments</li><li>Other reportable transfers between the entity and related persons</li></ul><p>The specific reporting treatment depends on the transaction and entity structure.</p><p>This is why bookkeeping for foreign-owned companies needs to capture more than ordinary revenue and expenses.</p><p>Owner and related-party activity should be identified clearly while it happens.</p><h2>Why Clean Books Are a Compliance Tool</h2><p>Bookkeeping is often viewed as a financial reporting function.</p><p>For foreign-owned businesses, it can also be an important part of compliance.</p><p>If owner contributions, withdrawals, reimbursements, intercompany transfers, and related-party payments are mixed together or incorrectly categorized, preparing required information returns becomes significantly more difficult.</p><p>Clean accounting records make it easier to determine:</p><ul><li>Who transferred money</li><li>Which entity received it</li><li>Why the transaction occurred</li><li>Whether it involved a related party</li><li>How it should be categorized</li><li>Whether additional reporting may be required</li></ul><p>This is one reason foreign-owner compliance should not be treated as a once-a-year tax exercise.</p><h2>The $25,000 Form 5472 Penalty</h2><p>The consequences of missing Form 5472 can be significant.</p><p>According to the IRS, a reporting corporation may be subject to a <strong>$25,000 penalty</strong> for failing to file a complete and correct Form 5472 by the required deadline.</p><p>A substantially incomplete Form 5472 can also be treated as a failure to file.</p><p>If the failure continues after the IRS provides notice, additional $25,000 continuation penalties can apply for each applicable 30-day period after the initial 90-day period. The IRS states that these continuation penalties have no maximum amount.</p><p>This is why a relatively small U.S. entity can still face a material compliance issue.</p><h2>The Problem Is Often Awareness, Not Intent</h2><p>Many reporting failures are not the result of businesses deliberately ignoring tax rules.</p><p>Foreign owners may simply misunderstand how U.S. entity classification works.</p><p>For example, an entrepreneur may believe:</p><p>“My LLC did not owe U.S. income tax, so there was nothing to file.”</p><p>But income tax liability and information-reporting requirements are not always the same thing.</p><p>Another common assumption is:</p><p>“The beneficial ownership requirement was eliminated, so foreign-owner reporting is no longer an issue.”</p><p>Again, the BOI rules administered by FinCEN and tax information reporting administered by the IRS are different systems.</p><p>The key is understanding which requirements apply to the specific entity.</p><h2>Foreign Ownership Requires a Different Compliance Lens</h2><p>A domestically owned small business and a foreign-owned U.S. company may look almost identical operationally.</p><p>They can have the same state registration, banking setup, customers, and accounting software.</p><p>Their federal reporting requirements, however, may differ.</p><p>Foreign ownership can introduce issues involving:</p><ul><li>Form 5472</li><li>Entity classification</li><li>Related-party reporting</li><li>Cross-border transactions</li><li>Recordkeeping requirements</li><li>Tax treaty considerations</li><li>Owner payments and distributions</li><li>U.S. trade or business questions</li></ul><p>Not every issue applies to every company.</p><p>That is exactly why foreign-owned businesses benefit from reviewing their structure and transactions rather than relying on generic small-business tax checklists.</p><h2>Compliance Should Start Before Filing Season</h2><p>Waiting until a filing deadline to identify foreign-owner issues can create unnecessary problems.</p><p>By that point, transactions may need to be reconstructed from bank statements, transfers may lack documentation, and important details about related-party activity may be difficult to recover.</p><p>A better approach is continuous compliance.</p><p>That means identifying important transactions as they occur and maintaining records throughout the year.</p><p>Companies can then approach tax preparation with organized information instead of trying to recreate an entire year's activity at the last minute.</p><h2>Review Owner Transfers Carefully</h2><p>Transfers between an owner and the business deserve particular attention.</p><p>Money moving into or out of an account does not automatically explain what the transaction represents.</p><p>A transfer could potentially be:</p><ul><li>A capital contribution</li><li>A distribution</li><li>A reimbursement</li><li>A loan</li><li>Payment for services</li><li>An intercompany transfer</li><li>Another form of related-party transaction</li></ul><p>The classification matters.</p><p>Financial records should document the purpose of these transactions rather than leaving them as generic transfers.</p><h2>Cross-Border Structures Add Another Layer</h2><p>Some foreign owners operate more than one entity.</p><p>For example, a U.S. company may interact with a foreign parent, sister company, shareholder, contractor, or other related organization.</p><p>Once money begins moving between related entities across borders, accounting and compliance can become considerably more complicated.</p><p>Businesses need to understand not only where money moved, but also:</p><ul><li>Which entities were involved</li><li>What the commercial purpose was</li><li>Whether the parties are related</li><li>How the transaction was documented</li><li>How it was recorded</li><li>Which reporting requirements may apply</li></ul><p>These details are much easier to manage when accounting systems are designed with foreign ownership in mind.</p><h2>Create a Compliance Clean Room</h2><p>The best time to identify a reporting problem is before it becomes a penalty notice.</p><p>A structured foreign-owner compliance review can examine the entity, ownership structure, historical filings, books, and related-party transactions to identify areas that may require attention.</p><p>Through <a href="https://garzonmeridian.com/clean-room/" target="_blank"><strong>The Clean Room at Garzon Meridian</strong></a>, foreign owners can review the financial and reporting structure surrounding their U.S. companies and identify potential compliance gaps before those gaps become more difficult to resolve.</p><p>The objective is not to create unnecessary complexity.</p><p>It is to determine what actually applies.</p><h2>Know What Changed — and What Didn't</h2><p>The U.S. reporting environment has changed significantly.</p><p>Federal BOI reporting for U.S.-created companies is no longer required under FinCEN's current rule.</p><p>But foreign owners should not interpret that change as the end of U.S. information-reporting responsibilities.</p><p>Form 5472 and related IRS requirements remain relevant for entities that fall within their scope, and the penalties for filing failures can be substantial.</p><p>For foreign-owned U.S. companies, the safest operating principle is simple:</p><p>Know your entity structure. Keep clean records. Identify related-party transactions as they occur. And determine which reporting requirements apply before the filing deadline arrives.</p><p>Strong compliance begins with visibility.</p>
