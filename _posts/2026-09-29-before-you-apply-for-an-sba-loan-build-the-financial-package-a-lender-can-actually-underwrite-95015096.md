<h1>Before You Apply for an SBA Loan: Build the Financial Package a Lender Can Actually Underwrite</h1><p>Getting ready for an SBA loan should begin before the application reaches a lender.</p><p>Many business owners approach financing by starting with the amount they want to borrow.</p><p>They think about:</p><ul><li>Purchase price</li><li>Expansion costs</li><li>Working capital</li><li>Equipment</li><li>Real estate</li><li>Down payment</li></ul><p>Those questions matter.</p><p>But the lender has another question first:</p><p><strong>Can the financial information prove that this business and borrower can support the proposed loan?</strong></p><p>That question changes the preparation process.</p><p>A strong financing package is not simply a collection of tax returns and bank statements.</p><p>It is a coherent financial story supported by records that can be traced, explained, and reconciled.</p><p>For companies with foreign ownership, related entities, owner transfers, or unusual cash flows, that preparation can become especially important.</p><h2>A Good Business Can Still Present a Difficult Loan File</h2><p>Owners sometimes assume that if the company is profitable, financing should be straightforward.</p><p>Profitability certainly matters.</p><p>But a lender also needs to understand how that profitability was calculated and whether the financial information is reliable.</p><p>Imagine two companies generating similar earnings.</p><h3>Company A</h3><p>Its books close consistently.</p><p>Owner activity is clearly classified.</p><p>Related-party transactions are documented.</p><p>Financial statements reconcile with underlying records.</p><p>Unusual expenses can be explained.</p><h3>Company B</h3><p>Its tax return shows strong income.</p><p>But the books contain:</p><ul><li>Unclassified transfers</li><li>Personal expenses</li><li>Old shareholder balances</li><li>Large miscellaneous categories</li><li>Multiple related entities</li><li>Inconsistent monthly reporting</li></ul><p>The businesses may be economically similar.</p><p>The second file simply requires much more work to understand.</p><p>That uncertainty can slow an underwriting process considerably.</p><h2>Lenders Need Evidence, Not Just a Story</h2><p>A founder may understand perfectly why money moved between two companies.</p><p>The lender does not have that institutional memory.</p><p>The file needs to make the explanation visible.</p><p>For example, if $100,000 entered the business from another entity, the lender may need to understand:</p><ul><li>Where it came from</li><li>Who controls the other entity</li><li>Why the transfer occurred</li><li>How it was recorded</li><li>Whether repayment is expected</li></ul><p>The owner saying:</p><p>“That's from my other company”</p><p>may be factually correct.</p><p>But underwriting relies on documentation.</p><p>This is one reason financing preparation should begin at the ledger level.</p><h2>Start With the Books</h2><p>Before preparing projections or presentation materials, make sure the historical accounting is reliable.</p><p>At a minimum, review:</p><ul><li>Bank reconciliations</li><li>Credit-card reconciliations</li><li>Revenue</li><li>Expenses</li><li>Payroll</li><li>Debt</li><li>Owner transactions</li><li>Related-party accounts</li><li>Balance-sheet balances</li></ul><p>The objective is to ensure that the financial statements can be traced back to actual transactions.</p><p>Garzon Meridian describes its own SBA preparation process as working from the ledger rather than simply creating presentation materials on top of existing numbers.</p><p>That distinction is important.</p><p>A polished spreadsheet cannot compensate for unreliable underlying accounting.</p><h2>Monthly Financials Need Consistency</h2><p>A lender reviewing several years of business performance may look for patterns.</p><p>That becomes difficult if every year or month uses a different accounting approach.</p><p>For example:</p><p>One year certain expenses are categorized as marketing.</p><p>The next year they appear under professional services.</p><p>Owner expenses move between several accounts.</p><p>Transfers are treated inconsistently.</p><p>Even when total profit is ultimately correct, inconsistent classification can make analysis harder.</p><p>A normalized monthly close helps create continuity.</p><h2>What Does “Normalize the Financials” Mean?</h2><p>Financial normalization involves identifying items that may distort the company's recurring operating performance.</p><p>Examples might include:</p><ul><li>One-time expenses</li><li>Unusual owner-related costs</li><li>Nonrecurring professional fees</li><li>Extraordinary events</li><li>Certain discretionary items</li></ul><p>Whether a specific item should be adjusted for underwriting purposes depends on the lender, transaction, documentation, and applicable rules.</p><p>The important point is that adjustments should be explainable.</p><p>A borrower should not simply remove expenses because doing so produces a better profit figure.</p><p>Every adjustment needs a defensible reason.</p><h2>Add-Backs Need Documentation</h2><p>The term “add-back” often appears during lending discussions.</p><p>An add-back generally refers to an expense that may be added back to reported earnings for analytical purposes because it is considered nonrecurring, discretionary, or otherwise treated differently by the lender.</p><p>This does not mean every undesirable expense can be removed.</p><p>Suppose a company spent $80,000 on an unusual legal matter that is not expected to recur.</p><p>A lender may want to understand:</p><ul><li>What happened</li><li>Why it was exceptional</li><li>Whether the expense is truly finished</li><li>Where it appears in the books</li><li>What documentation supports the explanation</li></ul><p>A clear normalization memo can make those adjustments much easier to evaluate.</p><p>Garzon Meridian includes an add-backs and normalization memo within its SBA Underwriting Room package.</p><h2>Debt Service Coverage Needs a Bridge to the Books</h2><p>One of the central questions in business lending is whether operating cash flow can support debt payments.</p><p>This is commonly discussed through debt service coverage.</p><p>But simply calculating a ratio is not enough.</p><p>The assumptions behind the calculation should connect back to the company's financial statements.</p><p>A useful bridge shows how the business moves from reported results to the cash-flow figure being used in underwriting.</p><p>That may require explaining:</p><ul><li>Reported earnings</li><li>Approved adjustments</li><li>Existing debt service</li><li>Proposed debt</li><li>Other relevant obligations</li></ul><p>Garzon Meridian's current SBA Readiness offering specifically includes a DSCR bridge as part of its lender-ready package.</p><h2>Why Source of Funds Matters</h2><p>When a borrower contributes equity to a transaction, the lender may need documentation showing where that money came from.</p><p>The funds may have accumulated through:</p><ul><li>Business income</li><li>Personal savings</li><li>Sale of an asset</li><li>Investment accounts</li><li>Other documented sources</li></ul><p>For cross-border owners, the trail can involve additional accounts or international transfers.</p><p>That makes organization particularly important.</p><p>A clean source-of-funds package should allow another person to follow the movement without relying on verbal explanations.</p><h2>Build the Trail Before the Money Moves</h2><p>Documentation is much easier when created contemporaneously.</p><p>Suppose an owner plans to move capital through several accounts before closing.</p><p>If the finance team understands the route in advance, it can preserve:</p><ul><li>Statements</li><li>Transfer confirmations</li><li>Account ownership evidence</li><li>Transaction dates</li><li>Explanations</li></ul><p>Trying to reconstruct the same path months later can be much more difficult.</p><h2>Foreign Ownership Can Add More Questions</h2><p>A U.S. operating company may be owned by:</p><ul><li>A foreign individual</li><li>A foreign company</li><li>Multiple international partners</li></ul><p>Again, foreign ownership does not inherently make the underlying business weak.</p><p>It can, however, make the financial structure more complex to document.</p><p>An underwriter may need to understand:</p><ul><li>Who owns the borrower</li><li>Which entities are related</li><li>Where funds originate</li><li>How related-party transactions work</li><li>Whether certain balances are loans, contributions, or something else</li></ul><p>Clarity matters.</p><h2>Intercompany Transfers Must Be Explained</h2><p>This is one of the most common areas where financial files become difficult to read.</p><p>Suppose a U.S. company sends money to a related foreign company.</p><p>The lender sees the wire.</p><p>What does it represent?</p><p>Possibilities might include:</p><ul><li>Service payment</li><li>Intercompany loan</li><li>Expense reimbursement</li><li>Distribution</li><li>Capital movement</li></ul><p>The transaction should be classified consistently with its actual substance and supported appropriately.</p><p>Large unexplained transfers create questions that can slow the review.</p><h2>Owner Transactions Deserve the Same Discipline</h2><p>Closely held companies often have financial activity involving their owners.</p><p>Examples can include:</p><ul><li>Owner contributions</li><li>Distributions</li><li>Shareholder loans</li><li>Reimbursements</li><li>Personal expenses paid by the company</li></ul><p>These transactions may be perfectly legitimate.</p><p>The problem arises when they are mixed into ordinary operations without clear classification.</p><p>Underwriting becomes easier when an analyst can quickly separate business performance from owner activity.</p><h2>This Is Where Financing Readiness Begins</h2><p>Once the historical accounting is clean and unusual activity is documented, the business can begin building the actual lending package.</p><p>A structured approach to <a href="https://garzonmeridian.com/sba-readiness/" target="_blank"><strong>SBA loan readiness</strong></a> helps move the process from simply collecting documents to creating a financial file where cash flow, equity sources, normalization adjustments, and related-party activity can be traced back to the underlying records.</p><p>That is a much stronger position than waiting for a lender to discover every inconsistency during underwriting.</p><h2>Do Not Wait Until the Application to Fix the Books</h2><p>The worst time to discover accounting problems is after financing has become urgent.</p><p>Perhaps the company already:</p><ul><li>Signed a purchase agreement</li><li>Found a location</li><li>Needs equipment</li><li>Has a seller waiting</li><li>Has a growth opportunity</li></ul><p>Suddenly the finance team has to reconstruct years of history under deadline pressure.</p><p>Preparing earlier creates more options.</p><p>It gives the business time to correct errors, collect missing documentation, and establish consistent monthly closes.</p><p>Garzon Meridian's SBA Readiness page explicitly advises borrowers to start preparing before they apply.</p><h2>Build a 13-Week Cash Forecast</h2><p>Historical financials demonstrate what the company has done.</p><p>A short-term cash forecast shows what may happen next.</p><p>A 13-week model can help management track expected:</p><ul><li>Collections</li><li>Payroll</li><li>Rent</li><li>Vendor payments</li><li>Taxes</li><li>Debt payments</li><li>Large purchases</li></ul><p>For financing preparation, this can also help identify whether the company has enough liquidity to navigate the period surrounding a transaction.</p><p>Garzon Meridian lists a 13-week cash forecast among the components of its current lender-ready package.</p><h2>Why 13 Weeks Can Be Useful</h2><p>Thirteen weeks represents roughly one quarter.</p><p>That time horizon is short enough to work with relatively concrete operating information but long enough to reveal emerging cash pressure.</p><p>Management can ask:</p><ul><li>Will cash tighten after closing?</li><li>Are there major tax payments approaching?</li><li>Does the business need more working capital?</li><li>Are collections slower than expected?</li></ul><p>This turns financing into part of the broader cash plan rather than an isolated transaction.</p><h2>Debt Should Fit the Operating Business</h2><p>Receiving financing is not the end goal.</p><p>The business needs to service the debt afterward.</p><p>That means management should evaluate the new obligation alongside:</p><ul><li>Existing debt</li><li>Payroll</li><li>Rent</li><li>Inventory</li><li>Tax obligations</li><li>Capital expenditures</li><li>Owner distributions</li></ul><p>A financing structure that looks manageable in isolation may become less comfortable when all obligations are viewed together.</p><h2>Do Not Ignore Post-Closing Monitoring</h2><p>Financial discipline should not disappear once the loan closes.</p><p>A business may still need to track:</p><ul><li>Cash</li><li>Debt service</li><li>Covenants where applicable</li><li>Profitability</li><li>Forecast performance</li><li>Working capital</li></ul><p>Garzon Meridian states that its SBA readiness model continues into covenant and cash monitoring after closing rather than ending with preparation of the application package.</p><p>This is a useful principle even regardless of provider.</p><p>Debt becomes part of the company's ongoing operating system.</p><h2>A Lender Package Should Tell One Consistent Story</h2><p>Imagine an underwriter reviewing:</p><ul><li>Tax returns</li><li>Monthly financial statements</li><li>Bank activity</li><li>Debt schedule</li><li>Source-of-funds documents</li><li>Owner information</li></ul><p>Those documents should reinforce one another.</p><p>Problems arise when:</p><p>The tax return says one thing.</p><p>The bookkeeping file says another.</p><p>The owner's explanation introduces a third interpretation.</p><p>Consistency does not mean every report looks identical.</p><p>It means differences can be explained.</p><h2>Prepare an Explanation Memo for Complexity</h2><p>If the company has unusual structures, do not force the analyst to reverse-engineer them.</p><p>A short memo can explain:</p><ul><li>Ownership</li><li>Related entities</li><li>Material intercompany flows</li><li>Major add-backs</li><li>Significant historical events</li><li>Owner transactions</li></ul><p>The memo should point back to supporting records.</p><p>Its purpose is not persuasion.</p><p>Its purpose is navigation.</p><h2>Make the Underwriter's Job Easier</h2><p>Borrowers often think underwriting is about convincing the bank that the company is excellent.</p><p>A better approach is to make the file easy to verify.</p><p>That means:</p><ul><li>Consistent records</li><li>Clear labels</li><li>Supporting documentation</li><li>Reconciled balances</li><li>Explanations for unusual activity</li><li>Organized files</li></ul><p>The more easily an underwriter can follow the financial story, the fewer basic questions may need to move back and forth.</p><h2>Organize Documents Before They Are Requested</h2><p>Create a structured document repository.</p><p>Depending on the transaction, folders might include:</p><p><strong>Financial Statements</strong></p><p><strong>Tax Returns</strong></p><p><strong>Ownership</strong></p><p><strong>Debt</strong></p><p><strong>Bank Statements</strong></p><p><strong>Source of Funds</strong></p><p><strong>Related Parties</strong></p><p><strong>Projections</strong></p><p><strong>Legal Documents</strong></p><p>The exact documents required will depend on the lender and transaction.</p><p>But creating a clean structure early can reduce scrambling later.</p><h2>Use Clear File Names</h2><p>This sounds minor.</p><p>It is not.</p><p>Avoid files such as:</p><p>“scan2398.pdf”</p><p>or:</p><p>“FINALfinal2.xlsx”</p><p>Use names that indicate exactly what each document contains.</p><p>For example:</p><p><strong>2025 Business Tax Return.pdf</strong></p><p><strong>2026-06 Balance Sheet.pdf</strong></p><p><strong>Equity Source - Bank Statement June 2026.pdf</strong></p><p>When many people review a file, organization saves time.</p><h2>Maintain Version Control</h2><p>Loan preparation can produce many versions of:</p><ul><li>Forecasts</li><li>Financial statements</li><li>Debt schedules</li><li>Normalization analyses</li></ul><p>Make it clear which version is current.</p><p>Otherwise, different participants may begin working from different numbers.</p><p>A single controlled data room or financial workspace helps reduce this problem.</p><h2>Reconcile Before Uploading</h2><p>Before providing financial statements, confirm that the underlying accounts are reconciled.</p><p>This applies particularly to:</p><ul><li>Cash</li><li>Credit cards</li><li>Debt</li><li>Intercompany balances</li></ul><p>If the financial statements change materially every time the lender asks a question, confidence in the file can deteriorate.</p><h2>Separate Recurring From Nonrecurring Events</h2><p>A lender evaluating repayment capacity wants to understand sustainable operating performance.</p><p>That makes it important to distinguish recurring operations from unusual events.</p><p>For example:</p><ul><li>One-time relocation</li><li>Extraordinary professional fees</li><li>Temporary shutdown</li><li>Unique project costs</li></ul><p>Again, documentation determines whether an adjustment is credible.</p><p>The objective is not to maximize add-backs.</p><p>It is to accurately explain the economics of the business.</p><h2>Prepare Management to Answer Questions</h2><p>The financial package should not be the only preparation.</p><p>Owners should understand their own numbers.</p><p>They should be able to explain:</p><ul><li>Revenue trends</li><li>Margins</li><li>Major expenses</li><li>Customer concentration</li><li>Debt</li><li>Cash needs</li><li>Related entities</li></ul><p>An owner who understands the financial story can respond more consistently when lenders ask follow-up questions.</p><h2>Know the Difference Between Cash and Profit</h2><p>This distinction becomes particularly important during underwriting.</p><p>A profitable company can still experience tight liquidity.</p><p>For example, profit may exist while cash is tied up in:</p><ul><li>Receivables</li><li>Inventory</li><li>Deposits</li><li>Capital expenditures</li></ul><p>The lender may therefore look beyond reported earnings.</p><p>Management should too.</p><h2>Watch Working Capital</h2><p>Growth can consume cash.</p><p>A business may win more customers and still need additional liquidity because it pays vendors before collecting from clients.</p><p>That makes working capital an important part of financing analysis.</p><p>Ask:</p><ul><li>How quickly do customers pay?</li><li>When must vendors be paid?</li><li>Is inventory increasing?</li><li>Does seasonality create cash gaps?</li></ul><p>These questions can affect how much financing the business actually needs.</p><h2>Do Not Borrow More Simply Because It Is Available</h2><p>The purpose of financing should be defined clearly.</p><p>Know:</p><ul><li>How much is needed</li><li>What it will fund</li><li>When the money will be used</li><li>How the loan will be repaid</li></ul><p>Debt should solve a business need.</p><p>It should not replace basic financial discipline.</p><h2>Financing Readiness Improves the Business Even Before a Loan</h2><p>One of the most useful aspects of preparing for underwriting is that the company often becomes financially stronger in the process.</p><p>Management may discover:</p><ul><li>Old balances</li><li>Poorly classified transactions</li><li>Missing documentation</li><li>Cash-flow weaknesses</li><li>Inconsistent reporting</li></ul><p>Correcting those issues can improve financial visibility regardless of whether the loan ultimately closes.</p><h2>Start Months Before the Capital Is Needed</h2><p>Ideally, financing preparation happens before urgency.</p><p>That creates time to:</p><ul><li>Clean historical books</li><li>Establish a monthly close</li><li>Document related-party activity</li><li>Build forecasts</li><li>Review cash requirements</li><li>Organize records</li></ul><p>A company that begins early can present its normal financial process.</p><p>A company that begins late may have to build the process specifically for the application.</p><h2>Lender-Ready Does Not Mean Guaranteed Approval</h2><p>No financial package can guarantee a particular lending decision.</p><p>Lenders evaluate numerous factors, and standards can vary by transaction and institution.</p><p>Preparation has a different objective.</p><p>It helps ensure the lender is evaluating the economic reality of the business rather than spending unnecessary time trying to resolve avoidable inconsistencies in the records.</p><p>That is an important distinction.</p><h2>A Strong File Begins Long Before the Bank Sees It</h2><p>SBA loan preparation is not fundamentally a document-collection exercise.</p><p>It is a financial systems exercise.</p><p>The business needs:</p><ul><li>Reliable books</li><li>Normalized financials</li><li>Documented adjustments</li><li>Clear related-party transactions</li><li>Traceable source of funds</li><li>Understandable cash flow</li><li>A forward-looking cash plan</li></ul><p>When those pieces already exist, assembling the lender package becomes much easier.</p><p>When they do not, financing preparation can turn into an emergency cleanup project.</p><p>For business owners planning to borrow, the best time to become lender-ready is before capital becomes urgent.</p><p>Build the financial story first.</p><p>Then give the lender the documentation needed to verify it.</p>
